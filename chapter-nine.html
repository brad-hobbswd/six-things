Chapter 9: A Historical Moment the Church Cannot Ignore

Religious Authority, Generational Traditions, and the Responsibility to Confront the Past

Church history is filled with accounts of faithfulness, sacrifice, perseverance, and extraordinary commitment to the teachings of Jesus Christ. Congregations have provided places of worship, communities have organized around shared beliefs, and generations of believers have worked to preserve their spiritual heritage. However, an honest examination of religious history must also acknowledge the ways human authority, cultural expectations, and institutional traditions have influenced the treatment of people within the Church. Remembering the past requires more than celebrating what previous generations accomplished; it also requires examining the practices that deserve reconsideration.

The history of American Christianity demonstrates that religious institutions have often reflected the social environments in which they developed. Churches have influenced education, family life, community organization, public morality, and social relationships. Their influence has sometimes provided protection and opportunity for people facing significant hardship, while certain religious institutions have also reinforced social divisions and expectations that were inconsistent with the dignity and equality of those they served. These realities require careful historical examination because religious language and institutional authority have not always been applied in ways that reflect the principles of Scripture.

The purpose of examining this history is not to establish that every congregation followed the same practices or that every religious leader participated in wrongdoing. Churches developed within different cultural, denominational, racial, and historical circumstances. Their experiences cannot responsibly be reduced to one narrative. Nevertheless, documented examples provide an opportunity to consider how religious authority can influence communities, how traditions acquire legitimacy, and how institutional practices can continue even when their consequences deserve examination.

When Religious Institutions Reflected Social Inequality

One of the clearest examples of the complicated relationship between American Christianity and social authority can be found in the history of slavery and racial segregation. During the eighteenth century, some slaveholders resisted efforts to evangelize enslaved people because they feared Christian conversion might encourage those individuals to recognize their equality with their owners. By 1706, legislation in at least six colonies had established that baptism did not automatically grant an enslaved person freedom. Enslaved Christians also faced restrictions on attending religious gatherings, leading many to establish secret worship meetings in cabins and surrounding woods. These gatherings provided opportunities for prayer, spiritual encouragement, and the preservation of religious identity under oppressive circumstances. Pew Research Center

The contradiction deserves attention because Christianity teaches the equal dignity of humanity before God, yet religious beliefs and institutions were sometimes used to support social arrangements that denied that dignity. The historical record also reveals that enslaved Christians developed their own expressions of faith, drawing encouragement from biblical accounts of deliverance and the promises of God. Their spiritual experiences demonstrate that genuine faith can survive even when religious institutions fail to uphold the principles they profess.

The establishment of independent Black Protestant denominations represents another important chapter in American religious history. In 1787, Richard Allen and other Black worshippers left a predominantly White Methodist congregation in Philadelphia after Black members were physically interrupted while praying in a section reserved for White worshippers. In 1816, Allen and representatives of other congregations established the African Methodist Episcopal Church, creating an institution through which Black Christians could worship and exercise religious leadership more freely. The African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church followed in 1821, while additional historically Black denominations emerged during the nineteenth century. Pew Research Center and Historic St. George’s United Methodist Church

These developments should be understood within their historical circumstances. Independent Black churches were not simply products of denominational preference; they emerged within a society where racial discrimination restricted participation and leadership in many religious institutions. Their congregations subsequently became important centers of education, community support, leadership development, and social organization. Recognizing this history allows us to understand how religious authority can either restrict human dignity or provide opportunities for communities to exercise their faith and independence.

The Pentecostal Movement and the Question of Authority

The early twentieth century provides another important example through the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles, which began in 1906 under the leadership of William J. Seymour, an African American preacher. The meetings attracted people from different racial and economic backgrounds, and the revival became an influential center for the development of Pentecostal Christianity. Worshippers emphasized the work of the Holy Spirit, spiritual gifts, holiness, and the experiences described in the book of Acts. The movement subsequently influenced Pentecostal congregations and denominations throughout the United States and internationally. Gastón Espinosa, William J. Seymour and the Origins of Global Pentecostalism

The history of Azusa Street also provides an opportunity to examine the relationship between spiritual experience and institutional authority. Women participated in the revival and exercised important ministerial responsibilities, including preaching, teaching, and evangelism. However, later developments within Pentecostal institutions raised questions about the extent of women’s participation in formal leadership and governing positions. Historical scholarship by Lisa P. Stephenson documents how women could be recognized for their spiritual gifts and ministerial contributions while remaining restricted from certain positions of institutional authority. Lisa P. Stephenson, “Prophesying Women and Ruling Men”

This history demonstrates that a movement can begin with considerable openness to spiritual participation while later institutional structures establish boundaries around who may exercise recognized authority. Such developments deserve examination without assuming that every denomination followed the same course or that every leadership decision resulted from the same motivation.

What History Requires Us to Examine

Historical examples cannot establish that every church has practiced discrimination, misused authority, or deliberately harmed members. They do, however, demonstrate why religious institutions must remain willing to examine their decisions, traditions, and treatment of people. The existence of a longstanding religious structure does not automatically establish that every aspect of that structure reflects biblical righteousness. History provides evidence of human decisions, while Scripture provides the standard by which believers evaluate their conduct.

Proverbs 6:16–19 offers a framework for understanding the spiritual responsibility involved. A proud look can influence how authority is exercised, a lying tongue can distort historical accounts, and false testimony can contribute to unjust treatment. The deliberate creation of division can also weaken communities that were established to provide fellowship and spiritual support. These connections should be approached carefully, recognizing that historical events require evidence and that identifying a biblical principle does not automatically establish the motives of every person involved.

The responsibility of the Church is not to become ashamed of every aspect of its history, nor should previous generations be judged collectively for decisions made under circumstances they may not have controlled. The responsibility is to remember honestly, preserve meaningful contributions, acknowledge documented failures, and ensure that harmful practices are not defended merely because they have become familiar.

A meaningful historical legacy requires more than remembering the people who built congregations and established ministries. It requires understanding the experiences of those who worshipped within those institutions, including individuals whose contributions were overlooked or whose participation was restricted. Their experiences belong within the broader account of American Christianity and deserve thoughtful consideration.

The historical record also reminds us that spiritual renewal must involve more than changes in worship practices, denominational structures, or public expressions of faith. A congregation may experience growth and recognition while still needing to examine how authority is exercised and how people are treated. Genuine renewal requires a willingness to evaluate personal and institutional conduct according to the principles of righteousness.

The Church cannot change the past, but every generation carries a responsibility for how that past is remembered and what lessons are carried forward. Historical awareness should produce wisdom rather than resentment, accountability rather than defensiveness, and a stronger commitment to the truth of God’s Word.

A faithful Church does not preserve history by concealing its failures; a faithful Church honors its spiritual inheritance by remaining willing to learn, correct, and grow.
