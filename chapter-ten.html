The Seven Things

Chapter 10: Let the Word Examine You First

Repentance, Accountability, and the Responsibility to Reflect Christ

There comes a point when every examination must move beyond identifying what is wrong and confront the responsibility to make things right. Throughout this book, we have examined seven behaviors that the Lord declares abominable, considered their influence upon human relationships, and explored how religious traditions and institutional practices can sometimes allow harmful conduct to continue. However, recognizing wrongdoing is only part of the responsibility. The greater question concerns what happens after the truth has been revealed. Are we willing to acknowledge our failures, accept correction, and make the necessary changes, or will we continue defending the very behaviors Scripture calls us to abandon?

Proverbs 6:16–19 does not provide an invitation to establish ourselves as judges over everyone around us. The passage demands that we recognize the seriousness of conduct that contradicts God’s righteousness. A proud look, a lying tongue, hands that shed innocent blood, a heart that devises wicked imaginations, feet that are swift in running to mischief, a false witness that speaks lies, and someone who sows discord among brethren represent seven areas of human behavior that deserve careful examination. Their presence within a religious community should never become less concerning simply because the individuals involved occupy respected positions or have established reputations.

Proverbs 6:16–19
16 These six things doth the LORD hate: yea, seven are an abomination unto him:
17 A proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood,
18 An heart that deviseth wicked imaginations, feet that be swift in running to mischief,
19 A false witness that speaketh lies, and he that soweth discord among brethren.

Personal accountability begins when we stop treating correction as an attack upon our identity. Nobody reaches a point in their spiritual journey where further instruction becomes unnecessary, and nobody develops a reputation so established that personal conduct no longer requires examination. The willingness to acknowledge wrongdoing is a demonstration of humility, not an admission that a person’s entire life has been without value. David demonstrated this principle when Nathan confronted him concerning his actions in 2 Samuel 12. Rather than dismissing the prophet’s message, David acknowledged his sin before the Lord. His response reminds us that genuine repentance begins when a person stops making excuses and accepts responsibility for their conduct.

The Church must also understand that repentance involves more than expressing regret. When wrongdoing has affected another person, meaningful accountability requires an honest acknowledgment of what happened and a willingness to address the consequences. An apology may open the door to reconciliation, but changed conduct demonstrates whether the person understands the seriousness of their actions. Luke 19:1–10 provides an example through the account of Zacchaeus, whose encounter with Jesus produced a willingness to make restitution to those he had wronged. His response demonstrated that a changed heart can influence the way a person handles previous decisions and accepts responsibility toward others.

Luke 19:1–10
1 And Jesus entered and passed through Jericho.
2 And, behold, there was a man named Zacchaeus, which was the chief among the publicans, and he was rich.
3 And he sought to see Jesus who he was; and could not for the press, because he was little of stature.
4 And he ran before, and climbed up into a sycomore tree to see him: for he was to pass that way.
5 And when Jesus came to the place, he looked up, and saw him, and said unto him, Zacchaeus, make haste, and come down; for to day I must abide at thy house.
6 And he made haste, and came down, and received him joyfully.
7 And when they saw it, they all murmured, saying, That he was gone to be guest with a man that is a sinner.
8 And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold.
9 And Jesus said unto him, This day is salvation come to this house, forsomuch as he also is a son of Abraham.
10 For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.

Those entrusted with leadership have a particular responsibility to demonstrate this willingness. A pastor who makes an incorrect decision should be able to acknowledge the mistake without treating accountability as a threat to spiritual authority. A longtime church member should be willing to reconsider an established practice when Scripture reveals a better direction. Parents and grandparents should recognize that acknowledging personal mistakes can provide younger generations with a meaningful example of humility. Leadership becomes more credible when those exercising influence demonstrate the same willingness to grow that they expect from others.

The responsibility of accountability also requires discernment when wrongdoing is reported. A congregation should establish appropriate procedures for addressing concerns, examining evidence, protecting vulnerable individuals, and ensuring that those involved receive fair consideration. Genuine misconduct must receive appropriate attention, while accusations must never be treated as established facts without responsible examination. Such safeguards protect the integrity of the congregation and demonstrate that truth matters more than reputation or personal loyalty. A commitment to righteousness requires both the courage to confront wrongdoing and the wisdom to avoid creating additional injustice through careless judgment.

Forgiveness and reconciliation also deserve careful consideration. Scripture repeatedly calls believers to forgive, yet forgiveness should never be confused with denying that harm occurred or requiring someone to return to circumstances where their well-being may be threatened. Reconciliation involves the willingness of those involved to address what happened, accept appropriate responsibility, and work toward restoring trust where possible. Some relationships may require time, boundaries, and demonstrated changes in conduct before restoration becomes appropriate. A church should encourage forgiveness without using spiritual instruction to silence people who need protection, truth, or responsible accountability.

The responsibility to examine ourselves extends beyond individual relationships and into the traditions we maintain. Every congregation should periodically consider whether established practices continue to reflect biblical instruction and whether the way authority is exercised supports the spiritual well-being of its members. Previous generations may have established customs with sincere intentions, but sincerity alone does not establish that every practice should remain unchanged. Younger generations must also approach inherited traditions with humility, recognizing that wisdom can be preserved through experiences that deserve careful consideration. The objective is not to establish one generation above another, but to ensure that every generation remains accountable to the same Word.

Jesus provides the clearest example of the character this examination should produce. His ministry demonstrated truth without deception, authority without arrogance, compassion without compromise, and correction without personal malice. He confronted hypocrisy, defended those who were treated unjustly, and taught His followers to approach others with humility. His example establishes that righteousness cannot be measured solely through religious participation or familiarity with Scripture. The character of Christ must influence the way believers handle disagreements, exercise authority, communicate truth, and respond when another person needs correction.

The seven things identified by Solomon therefore leave every reader with a responsibility that cannot be transferred to someone else. We must examine the attitudes we have allowed to develop, the words we have spoken, the intentions we have entertained, and the influence our decisions have had upon others. Such examination should not end when the final page of this book has been read. The principles of Proverbs 6:16–19 deserve continued attention because the responsibility to live righteously remains part of the Christian walk.

The Church does not need another generation that merely becomes skilled at identifying the failures of others. We need believers who understand the importance of personal integrity, leaders who welcome appropriate accountability, and congregations that value truth more than appearances. We need older members willing to share wisdom without demanding unquestioned authority, younger believers willing to receive instruction without surrendering discernment, and individuals who understand that unity must be established through righteousness rather than personal control.

The Word of God has not lost its authority, and the responsibility of those who profess faith in Jesus Christ has not diminished. The seven things remain serious because the character of God has not changed. Our responsibility is to approach Scripture with enough humility to recognize that the examination applies to us just as surely as we believe that same Scripture applies to everyone else.

Before asking whether someone else has lived according to the Word of God, ask whether your own life reflects the Word you profess to believe.
