The Seven Things

Chapter 4: Holiness Without Humanity

When Religious Appearance Becomes More Important Than the Condition of the Heart

Holiness is central to the Christian life, representing a call to live in a manner that reflects the character and purposes of God. Scripture repeatedly instructs believers to pursue righteousness, exercise self-control, and separate themselves from conduct that contradicts His Word. However, the pursuit of holiness must never become disconnected from the qualities that demonstrate God’s character, particularly mercy, compassion, humility, and love. A person may become highly attentive to outward religious expectations while neglecting the inward transformation that gives those expectations their proper meaning. When appearance becomes the primary evidence of spirituality, the deeper responsibility of living righteously can become obscured.

Throughout religious history, communities have established standards concerning dress, worship, personal conduct, and participation in congregational life. Some standards have been rooted in sincere biblical convictions, while others have developed through denominational customs, cultural expectations, and interpretations passed from one generation to another. Such practices deserve thoughtful consideration rather than automatic acceptance or rejection. The important distinction is whether a particular expectation reflects a sound biblical principle or whether human preference has gradually acquired the authority of divine instruction.

Jesus addressed this distinction when He confronted religious leaders who carefully maintained outward appearances while neglecting the more substantial responsibilities of their faith. In Matthew 23:23, He spoke directly about their attention to tithing while neglecting the weightier matters of the law, including judgment, mercy, and faith. His words revealed that religious precision in one area does not compensate for neglect in another. A person cannot establish genuine righteousness through outward observance while disregarding the principles that should govern their treatment of others.

Matthew 23:23
23 Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith: these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone.

This teaching carries particular importance when examining the seven things identified in Proverbs 6:16–19. A proud look can exist beneath an appearance of religious dedication, and a lying tongue does not become acceptable simply because its owner participates in worship. The outward expression of faith must be consistent with the inward character God desires. Religious activity may demonstrate involvement within a congregation, but genuine spiritual maturity must also be evident through honesty, humility, justice, and responsible conduct.

The relationship between holiness and compassion is especially important because Scripture does not present them as opposing qualities. Micah 6:8 identifies what the Lord requires: doing justly, loving mercy, and walking humbly with God. These instructions establish a balanced understanding of righteousness in which personal conduct and the treatment of others remain inseparable. Mercy does not require overlooking wrongdoing, and compassion does not eliminate moral responsibility. Rather, both should influence how believers approach people, address difficult circumstances, and exercise discernment.

Micah 6:8
8 He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?

The danger of emphasizing religious appearance without corresponding character is that people can become preoccupied with measuring the visible conduct of others while overlooking the responsibilities Scripture places upon themselves. A congregation may devote considerable attention to maintaining established standards while giving insufficient attention to honesty, fairness, and the dignity of those within its care. When such priorities become established, the outward appearance of religious order can remain intact even while the relationships within the congregation suffer.

James 1:26–27 offers another important perspective by connecting genuine religious practice with the responsible use of speech and concern for those experiencing vulnerability. James explains that a person who considers themselves religious but fails to control their tongue deceives their own heart. He also connects pure and undefiled religion with caring for orphans and widows in their distress and maintaining personal integrity. His instruction demonstrates that genuine devotion cannot be separated from the way people speak, the way they treat others, and the responsibilities they accept toward those in need.

James 1:26–27
26 If any man among you seem to be religious, and bridleth not his tongue, but deceiveth his own heart, this man's religion is vain.
27 Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world.

This examination should also remind church leaders that holiness is not established through the demands placed upon a congregation alone. Those entrusted with teaching and spiritual oversight must demonstrate the same principles they expect others to follow. A pastor who teaches humility must remain teachable, a church mother who encourages godly speech must exercise care with her own words, and a longtime member who emphasizes Christian character must demonstrate integrity through personal conduct. Leadership carries an obligation to model the instruction being communicated, because influence becomes meaningful when accompanied by consistent example.

The Church does not need to abandon standards of holiness to demonstrate compassion, nor must believers compromise biblical convictions to treat people with dignity. Both responsibilities can exist together when Scripture remains the foundation of personal conduct. The challenge is to recognize when religious expectations have become disconnected from the very character they were intended to encourage. A congregation should be known for its commitment to truth and righteousness, but those qualities should also be visible in its patience, fairness, hospitality, and treatment of people.

Holiness should be evident not only in what we refuse to participate in, but also in the character we demonstrate toward others.
