The Seven Things

Introduction

Before You Point Your Finger

There are passages of Scripture that comfort the heart, strengthen the weary, and remind us of the goodness of God. There are also passages that confront our character, challenge our conduct, and require us to examine the condition of our hearts. Proverbs 6:16–19 belongs to the latter. In these verses, Solomon identifies seven things that the Lord hates, exposing behaviors that stand in direct opposition to His righteousness. Among them are pride, dishonesty, wicked intentions, false testimony, and the deliberate sowing of discord. These are serious matters, deserving more than a passing acknowledgment or a convenient application to somebody else’s life.

Somewhere within religious culture, the examination of Scripture has sometimes become more focused on identifying the failures of others than recognizing the failures within ourselves. Words intended to teach righteousness can become instruments of shame, judgment, and division when they are used without humility, compassion, or personal accountability. The very behaviors condemned in Scripture can find their way into the lives of those who confidently quote its pages.

This deserves particular attention within traditional church environments, where generations of pastors, church mothers, elders, deacons, and longtime members have helped shape religious expectations and practices. Many have faithfully served, sacrificed, prayed, and preserved meaningful traditions. Their contributions deserve recognition. However, respect for previous generations must never become an excuse to overlook harmful conduct or place longstanding customs beyond biblical examination. What has been passed down deserves thoughtful consideration, and what contradicts God’s Word deserves correction.

The purpose of this book is not to attack a generation, discredit the Church, or dismiss the importance of spiritual leadership. Rather, we will examine the seven behaviors identified in Proverbs 6:16–19 and consider how they have appeared within religious communities, how certain practices have been normalized across generations, and how Scripture can be misused to hurt people rather than encourage righteousness. We will explore the historical and contemporary consequences of pride, dishonesty, malicious intentions, false accusations, and division while maintaining a commitment to biblical truth.

Every generation must be willing to participate in this examination. Pastors must examine their leadership, longtime members must examine their influence, and younger believers must examine their own conduct. Years of service, religious titles, denominational traditions, and personal reputations do not exempt anyone from accountability before God.

The seven things are not seven accusations to direct toward people we dislike. They are seven serious warnings that demand personal reflection and spiritual responsibility. Before we point toward another person’s shortcomings, we must be willing to consider what the Word of God reveals about our own.

The question this book asks is simple, yet deeply challenging: Are we living according to the Word we so readily use to correct everyone else?

Jesus described the worship the Father seeks as worship offered in spirit and in truth. His words in John 4:23–24 place the life of faith beyond outward performance or inherited religious form. The truth of God is not made alive through human authority, personal preference, or repeated custom; the Spirit of God leads people into a genuine response to what God has revealed. The purpose of examining Proverbs is therefore not merely to identify conduct that deserves condemnation. The purpose is to allow the Word to shape the living character of those who receive it, so that truth is practiced among the people who are still here to hear, learn, repent, and grow.

John 4:23–24 But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.

James gives this same responsibility a practical form when he warns against hearing the Word without doing what it teaches. Scripture is not fulfilled in a person’s life merely through familiarity with its language. Its instruction must move from hearing into conduct. That principle will guide the pages that follow: the reader is invited to consider the meaning of the text, the history surrounding its use, and the responsibility of living according to its truth.

James 1:22 But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.

Let the examination begin.
