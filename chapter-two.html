The Seven Things

Chapter 2: When the Mirror Becomes a Weapon

The Misuse of Scripture and the Refusal of Self-Examination

Scripture carries the authority to teach, correct, guide, and expose the condition of the human heart. Its purpose is not determined by personal preference or the convenience of the person quoting it. Yet the way Scripture is presented and applied can reveal whether someone genuinely desires righteousness or simply wants to establish authority over another person. When biblical instruction becomes a means of humiliation, intimidation, or personal condemnation, the message of Scripture is being handled without the humility its application requires.

The difficulty often begins when people become more comfortable identifying wrongdoing in others than examining their own conduct. A person may know numerous passages concerning holiness, obedience, submission, and sin while remaining unwilling to consider whether their own behavior reflects those same principles. Scripture then becomes something they hold over others rather than something they allow to govern their own lives. The problem is not the authority of the Word, but the attitude of someone who believes the responsibility to receive correction belongs primarily to everyone else.

Jesus addressed this condition directly in Matthew 7:1–5 when He taught about judgment and the responsibility to examine oneself before attempting to remove the speck from another person’s eye. His illustration of the beam and the speck was intentional. He exposed the contradiction of someone attempting to correct another person’s small imperfection while remaining unwilling to acknowledge a much greater problem within their own life. Jesus was not eliminating the responsibility to exercise discernment or address wrongdoing. He was confronting hypocrisy and establishing that personal examination must precede the correction of others.

Matthew 7:1–5
1 Judge not, that ye be not judged.
2 For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.
3 And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?
4 Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye?
5 Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.

This distinction matters because biblical accountability is necessary within every healthy Christian community. Churches must address misconduct, leaders must exercise discernment, and believers must be willing to correct one another when circumstances require such intervention. However, correction must be grounded in truth, humility, and a genuine desire for restoration. Galatians 6:1 instructs those who are spiritual to restore someone overtaken in a fault with a spirit of meekness, while considering themselves lest they also be tempted. The instruction places responsibility upon the person offering correction, reminding them that spiritual maturity should influence both the message and the manner in which that message is delivered.

Galatians 6:1
1 Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.

A significant concern arises when religious language is used to give personal opinions the appearance of divine authority. Someone may disagree with another person’s decision, interpretation, or circumstances and then present that disagreement as though God Himself has issued a judgment. Personal convictions can be meaningful, and biblical principles should influence the decisions of believers, but neither personal preference nor denominational custom should automatically be elevated to the authority of Scripture. When individuals fail to distinguish between biblical instruction and personal expectations, they risk placing burdens upon others that God has not commanded.

Jesus confronted a similar problem among religious leaders in Matthew 23:1–4. He addressed those who placed burdens upon others while failing to demonstrate the responsibility and integrity their teaching required. His criticism was directed toward the contradiction between public religious authority and personal conduct. The passage remains important because it demonstrates that possessing a position of influence does not guarantee that a person’s actions reflect the message they teach. Leadership carries responsibility, and those who instruct others must remain accountable to the same principles they expect others to follow.

Matthew 23:1–4
1 Then spake Jesus to the multitude, and to his disciples,
2 Saying, The scribes and the Pharisees sit in Moses’ seat:
3 All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works: for they say, and do not.
4 For they bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne, and lay them on men’s shoulders; but they themselves will not move them with one of their fingers.

Within traditional church culture, this issue can become particularly complicated when religious authority is closely connected to age, reputation, family history, or years of membership. A longtime church member may be respected for their service, and a pastor may carry responsibilities that deserve appropriate regard. However, respect should never become an agreement to overlook wrongdoing. A person’s history of faithful service deserves acknowledgment, but previous contributions cannot serve as permanent protection from accountability. The biblical expectation of humility applies just as much to those who have occupied positions of influence for decades as it does to someone who has only recently joined a congregation.

The misuse of Scripture also becomes apparent when passages are selected to support a predetermined conclusion while other relevant biblical instructions are ignored. A person may emphasize submission without addressing the responsibility of leaders to serve with humility. They may demand forgiveness without acknowledging the need for repentance, or speak about unity while refusing to address the conduct that has damaged relationships. Such selective application creates an incomplete understanding of biblical responsibility because Scripture must be approached with respect for its context and the full counsel of God’s instruction.

The account of the woman brought before Jesus in John 8:1–11 offers another important perspective. The religious leaders presented her circumstances as an opportunity to test Jesus, placing the emphasis upon her offense while their own intentions remained concealed. Jesus responded in a way that exposed the responsibility of those bringing the accusation. His words redirected attention toward the hearts of the accusers before He addressed the woman. The account does not suggest that wrongdoing should be celebrated or that moral responsibility should be dismissed. Rather, it demonstrates that those who presume to judge another person must also recognize their own need for righteousness and mercy.

John 8:1–11
1 Jesus went unto the mount of Olives.
2 And early in the morning he came again into the temple, and all the people came unto him; and he sat down, and taught them.
3 And the scribes and Pharisees brought unto him a woman taken in adultery; and when they had set her in the midst,
4 They say unto him, Master, this woman was taken in adultery, in the very act.
5 Now Moses in the law commanded us, that such should be stoned: but what sayest thou?
6 This they said, tempting him, that they might have to accuse him. But Jesus stooped down, and with his finger wrote on the ground, as though he heard them not.
7 So when they continued asking him, he lifted up himself, and said unto them, He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her.
8 And again he stooped down, and wrote on the ground.
9 And they which heard it, being convicted by their own conscience, went out one by one, beginning at the eldest, even unto the last: and Jesus was left alone, and the woman standing in the midst.
10 When Jesus had lifted up himself, and saw none but the woman, he said unto her, Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee?
11 She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more.

The danger of using Scripture as a weapon is that the person applying the passage may begin to confuse personal authority with divine authority. Once that distinction disappears, correction can become a means of controlling conversations, protecting reputations, and discouraging questions. People may become more concerned with avoiding the displeasure of influential members than understanding what Scripture actually teaches. In such circumstances, the appearance of religious order can be preserved while the deeper responsibilities of truth, justice, and humility receive insufficient attention.

The answer is not to abandon correction or treat every challenge to authority as evidence of mistreatment. Churches need accountability, and believers need the freedom to address legitimate concerns through appropriate and truthful means. However, the responsibility to receive correction must never be separated from the responsibility to offer correction properly. A congregation that expects humility from its members must also expect humility from its leadership, and a person who teaches biblical standards must be willing to live under those same standards.

The words of Proverbs 6:16–19 therefore remain relevant to this discussion. A proud look can influence the way someone approaches another person’s shortcomings, a lying tongue can distort the circumstances surrounding a disagreement, and a false witness can turn personal assumptions into damaging accusations. The sowing of discord can follow when such conduct becomes accepted within a community. The seven things are not merely subjects for theological discussion; they provide a serious standard against which the conduct of every believer should be examined.

Proverbs 6:16–19
16 These six things doth the LORD hate: yea, seven are an abomination unto him:
17 A proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood,
18 An heart that deviseth wicked imaginations, feet that be swift in running to mischief,
19 A false witness that speaketh lies, and he that soweth discord among brethren.

Scripture should never become a shield against personal accountability or a weapon for establishing superiority over another person. Its authority is greatest in our lives when we are willing to receive its correction before demanding obedience from someone else.
