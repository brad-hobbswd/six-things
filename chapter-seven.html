The Seven Things

Chapter 7: Who Gave You the Authority?

Spiritual Leadership, Accountability, and the Misuse of Influence

Spiritual leadership carries a responsibility that extends far beyond occupying a position, holding a title, or receiving recognition from a congregation. Those entrusted with guiding God’s people are expected to demonstrate the character, humility, and integrity reflected in the teachings of Jesus Christ. Leadership within the Church is not an opportunity to establish personal superiority or exercise unrestricted control over others. It is a responsibility to serve, teach, protect, and provide guidance according to the principles of Scripture. When the purpose of leadership becomes separated from those responsibilities, the authority intended to support the spiritual well-being of others can become a source of unnecessary harm.

Throughout Scripture, authority is presented as something that must be exercised with responsibility. Jesus demonstrated this principle when His disciples began discussing which among them should be considered the greatest. In Mark 10:42–45, He contrasted the behavior of rulers who exercised authority over others with the kind of leadership expected among His followers. He explained that greatness within His kingdom would be demonstrated through service rather than domination. His instruction challenged the assumption that influence should produce personal importance and established a different standard for those who would lead in His name.

Mark 10:42–45
42 But Jesus called them to him, and saith unto them, Ye know that they which are accounted to rule over the Gentiles exercise lordship over them; and their great ones exercise authority upon them.
43 But so shall it not be among you: but whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister:
44 And whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all.
45 For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.

This teaching remains essential when examining traditional church structures, where certain positions have historically carried considerable influence. Pastors, elders, deacons, church mothers, and other established leaders may become trusted voices within a congregation, and their decisions can affect the spiritual experiences and personal lives of many people. Such influence deserves respect when exercised responsibly, but the presence of a title does not establish that every decision is correct. Leadership must remain accountable because authority is entrusted for a purpose rather than granted as personal ownership over the lives of others.

First Peter 5:2–3 provides direct instruction concerning this responsibility. Peter encourages elders to shepherd the flock of God willingly and eagerly, without pursuing personal gain or exercising authority as though they were masters over those entrusted to their care. The passage establishes that spiritual oversight should be characterized by willingness, integrity, and godly example. A leader’s responsibility is to guide people toward Christ, not to make their personal approval the foundation of someone’s faith. The congregation belongs to God, and those who serve within positions of leadership remain accountable to Him for the manner in which they fulfill their responsibilities.

1 Peter 5:2–3
2 Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind;
3 Neither as being lords over God's heritage, but being ensamples to the flock.

One of the difficulties that can develop within religious environments is the confusion between biblical submission and unquestioning obedience to human authority. Scripture teaches believers to respect those who labor among them and provide spiritual oversight, but such instruction does not establish that leaders are beyond examination. Acts 17:11 commends the Bereans because they received the message with eagerness while examining the Scriptures daily to determine whether what they were hearing was true. Their example demonstrates that spiritual attentiveness includes discernment. Listening respectfully and examining what is taught are responsibilities that can exist together.

Acts 17:11
11 These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.

The misuse of authority can become particularly damaging when individuals use their positions to discourage legitimate questions or protect personal interests. A leader may begin interpreting disagreement as rebellion, questions as disrespect, or accountability as an attack upon their calling. Such reactions can create an environment where people become more concerned with maintaining a leader’s approval than understanding biblical instruction. However, genuine spiritual authority does not require the absence of questions, nor does responsible leadership depend upon preventing others from expressing legitimate concerns.

The warning concerning pride in Proverbs 6:16 is especially relevant here. Leadership can become dangerous when a person’s position begins influencing their perception of personal importance. Years of ministry, public recognition, denominational standing, and a history of service can gradually become sources of personal identity rather than reminders of responsibility. Yet no amount of experience places a person above the standards of Scripture. The greater the influence entrusted to someone, the greater the importance of exercising that influence with humility and integrity.

Accountability must also be understood correctly. Holding leaders responsible does not mean treating every disagreement as evidence of misconduct or assuming that every difficult decision represents an abuse of authority. Churches must maintain appropriate structures for addressing concerns, evaluating conduct, protecting vulnerable individuals, and ensuring that allegations receive fair consideration. Such processes should protect the congregation while respecting the rights and dignity of everyone involved. Accountability is most meaningful when established procedures are applied consistently rather than according to someone’s popularity, influence, or personal relationships.

The example of Jesus provides the clearest standard for understanding leadership. Throughout His ministry, He demonstrated compassion toward people who were overlooked, challenged religious hypocrisy, and confronted those who used their influence in ways that burdened others. His authority was never dependent upon humiliating people or demanding personal admiration. He taught with conviction, served with humility, and remained faithful to the purpose for which He came. Those who lead within His Church should consider whether their conduct reflects the character of the One whose name they represent.

This responsibility extends beyond pastors and formally appointed leaders. Every person who holds influence within a congregation must recognize that words and decisions can affect others. A longtime member who controls access to relationships, a church mother whose opinions shape how others are received, or a ministry leader who influences the treatment of volunteers all carry responsibilities that deserve thoughtful consideration. Formal titles may differ, but the obligation to demonstrate honesty, fairness, and humility remains the same.

The question of authority must therefore begin with the recognition that leadership belongs to God as a responsibility entrusted to human beings. Those who serve within the Church should never confuse being respected with being unquestionable, or being entrusted with responsibility with possessing unlimited control. Spiritual leadership is demonstrated through faithful service, responsible decision-making, and a willingness to receive correction when circumstances require it.

The authority to lead within the Church is a responsibility to serve God’s people, not permission to place oneself above the accountability of God’s Word.
